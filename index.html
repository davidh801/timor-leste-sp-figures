Table 1: Sample composition

Purposive sample from 14 workshops across three sites in October and November 2022. Analytical-sample sizes per model shown at the bottom.

N = 282. 58.5% women; median age 39 (IQR 27 to 58); 32.6% aged 30 or below; 14.5% veteran-household; 31.6% reported one or more difficulties. Site-level N pending workshop-to-site lookup.

Table A: Seven deservingness items

Five CARIN items (Control, Attitude, Reciprocity, Identity, Need) plus two extension items (state responsibility, individualism).

ItemConstruct and referencesQuestion wordingScale
self_helpIndividual responsibility / self-reliance orientation. Closest to CARIN Attitude as operationalised in Meuleman, Roosma and Abts (2020) but framed at the population rather than claimant level. See also Kootstra (2016).“Everyone should take care of themselves”Strongly disagree to Strongly agree
workCanonical CARIN Control (van Oorschot 2000; Meuleman, Roosma and Abts 2020): whether recipients are able to be self-supporting. Reeskens and van Oorschot (2013) show Control and Attitude commonly co-move.“If people can work, then they do not need to be helped”Strongly disagree to Strongly agree
neigh_helpCommunity-level solidarity, closest to CARIN Identity (van Oorschot 2000): the moral obligation to help those close to us.“People should help their neighbours if they are in need”Strongly disagree to Strongly agree
govt_helpState-responsibility orientation. Not part of CARIN. Extension item drawn from the wider welfare-state legitimacy literature (Roosma, Gelissen and van Oorschot 2013).“Government has a responsibility to citizens if they are in need”Strongly disagree to Strongly agree
own_needsIndividualism / self-prioritisation orientation. Not part of CARIN. Extension item complementing others_needs (see Kootstra 2016 on self-interest as a distinct dimension).“Mostly I put my own needs first, instead of the needs of others”Strongly disagree to Strongly agree
others_needsOther-directedness / altruism. Related to CARIN Need (van Oorschot 2000) but framed from the respondent’s side (their disposition to help) rather than the recipient’s severity of deprivation.“I like to help others, if they are in need”Strongly disagree to Strongly agree
returnCanonical CARIN Reciprocity (van Oorschot 2000; Meuleman, Roosma and Abts 2020): the moral expectation that giving generates a return. Central to the paper’s H1.“If I do something for someone else, I want something in return”Strongly disagree to Strongly agree

Items enter m_likert_items as mean-centred continuous predictors on R1 shares (all seven together, so any single coefficient is net of the other six). Not standardised, not aggregated.

Figure: Distribution of the seven deservingness items

Diverging Likert bars for each of the seven pre-experiment deservingness items. Red = disagree, blue = agree, grey = neutral (split at centre).

Four items sit near the agree-side ceiling: others_needs (94.9% agree), neigh_help (91.7%), self_help (90.4%), work (90.4%). Return divides the sample: 49.4% disagree, 42.1% agree. Instrumental reciprocity is not the dominant framing in the sample.

Table B: Six governance and trust items

Four Q15 leadership items (municipality and national, satisfaction and trust) plus two Q16 government-decision items (fairness and interests).

ItemConstruct and referencesQuestion wordingScale
satis_mun_leaderSatisfaction with local political leadership. Subjective evaluation of the incumbent’s performance at the municipality level. Standard measure in political-support research (Norris 2011).“Satisfaction with current municipality leadership”Very low to Very high
trust_mun_leaderInstitutional trust at the local level. Distinguished from satisfaction as a more diffuse, less performance-contingent orientation (Marien 2011). Hartzell, Hoddie and Page (2024) show trust in political authorities in fragile settings varies with perceived fairness.“Trust in current municipality leadership”Very low to Very high
satis_nat_leaderSame construct as Q15a at the national level.“Satisfaction with current national leadership”Very low to Very high
trust_nat_leaderSame construct as Q15b at the national level.“Trust in current national leadership”Very low to Very high
govt_dec_fairPerceived procedural fairness of government decision-making. Distinguished from satisfaction and trust: emphasises whether decisions are reached through legitimate procedures. Tyler (2006) on procedural justice; Mcloughlin (2015) on the fairness-legitimacy relationship in fragile settings.“The government makes fair decisions”Strongly disagree to Strongly agree
govt_dec_intPerceived responsiveness: whether government decisions serve the respondent’s interests. Distinct from procedural fairness (outcome-side rather than process-side). Related to the substantive-representation dimension of political legitimacy.“Government decisions usually serve my interests”Strongly disagree to Strongly agree

Cronbach’s alpha for the six-item composite is 0.854 (Q15 sub-set 0.886; Q16 sub-set 0.676). Individual items enter m_fair_alloc and m_fair_only; the composite state_legit (mean of z-scored items) enters m_legit_only and m_legit_carin.

Figure: Distribution of the six governance and trust items

Diverging Likert bars for satisfaction and trust in municipality and national leadership (Q15a-d), plus government fair-decisions and serves-my-interests (Q16a-b).

All six items skew positive but not overwhelmingly. Trust runs higher than satisfaction on both muni and national side. Government fair decisions (Q16a) and serves my interests (Q16b) are the most sceptical of the six, with 21% and 27% disagreeing respectively.

Figure: Citizen allocations vs government spending, aggregated

Four-bucket comparison. Citizen R1 aggregate share of the $400 budget vs the government R4-implied share of the per-household basket.

Government puts 87% of its per-household basket into veterans; citizens allocate 7%. Citizens direct 22% to categories with no formal scheme cited in R4 (Family, HH w/ sick, Youth w/ siblings, Social-security fam).

#fig_citizen_vs_govt_aggregate

Figure: Citizen allocations vs government per-household spending, by category

Per-household detail across the 15 recipient categories. Red bars = citizen R1 mean allocation (share × $400 budget); grey bars = government monthly payment for that category per the R4 script.

Government payments for the three veteran categories are $425 per household; citizens allocate roughly $8 to $11 to each. Citizens allocate more than the government pays for pregnant single ($56 vs $15), single disabled ($52 vs $30), and single mother ($39 vs $20). Elderly categories are roughly matched at $30 to $35 either side. Error bars are 95% CIs on the citizen mean (n = 267 with complete R1 data).
